Savarkar’s militarisation policy and Pakistan supporters’ opposition

Consolidated additions: S16–S25 · Research dossier • 18 October 2026 • Printed page numbers distinguished from PDF image numbers

The strongest evidence supports the narrower proposition requested: Savarkar deliberately sought to change the communal balance of military power; Jinnah publicly identified that policy as a threat to Muslim political independence and attacked it; a contemporary Mahasabha pamphlet reproduces unusually explicit Muslim newspaper anxiety about Hindu and Sikh recruitment. Pro-Pakistan writing associated with Jinnah also warned that a united India could dilute northwestern military representation.

These findings establish intention, perception and opposition. They do not require a claim that Savarkar alone changed army recruitment, or that his policy made Pakistan objectively impossible. The latter distinction matters because the sources show hostile criticism and fear of a possible outcome, while Pakistan was eventually created.

1. The clearest direct evidence: Jinnah at Nagpur, 26 December 1941

S1 is a scan of a collection published in Lahore in January 1942, immediately after the speech. Its imprint was checked visually. The relevant passage appears under “Mahasabha Ambition” on printed p.308 and “The Whole” on p.309.

Jinnah names Savarkar and Moonje and explicitly identifies the Mahasabha’s programme: militarise Hindus, encourage entry into the army, navy and air force, and support the war. He challenges their motives, depicts their cooperation as instrumental, and attributes to them the expectation that Pakistan will evaporate into the air. He asks whether such leaders should be locked up somewhere.

On the following page he describes an imagined outcome in which Hindus acquire 75 per cent representation across the armed services and administration. Hindu garrisons would then hold the northwestern and northeastern frontiers under a central government, preventing Muslim India from becoming an independent state with its own armed forces. His tone is openly derisive, including the sarcastic description of Moonje as a field marshal.

What this proves: opposition from the foremost Pakistan advocate to this particular programme; recognition of its strategic purpose; and a direct connection, in his argument, between Hindu military predominance and obstruction of Muslim statehood. It is stronger than a retrospective admirer’s explanation of what Savarkar supposedly intended.

Limit: Jinnah describes the Mahasabha’s hoped-for future, while dismissing it as a dream and folly. It would overstate the evidence to say he conceded that Pakistan was already defeated. “Hostile public opposition, informed by fear of its implications” is a defensible description. “Admission of inevitable failure” is not.

2. A substantial newspaper lead: Eastern Times, reproduced in February 1943

S2, Bhide’s 1945 pamphlet, contains chapter X, “Magic of Hindu Militarisation Movement,” printed pp.26–28. The chapter is dated February 1943. This is the source behind Keer’s later quotation: Keer, p.259 note 11, points specifically to Bhide p.27.

Bhide attributes a lengthy passage to the Lahore Muslim newspaper The Eastern Times. The passage objects to recruitment from all classes and places following relaxation of the distinction between military and non-military races. It calls this a great injustice to Muslims, discusses the Sikhs’ and Jats’ recruitment and the Khalsa Defence of India Association, and then credits the Hindu Mahasabha with substantial recruitment success and increased prestige.

The sharpest expression is: From our Muslim view point, all these conditions are most disastrous. The quotation then urges Muslims to join the army, rejects the argument that military enlistment merely means selling oneself for money, and asks why Muslims lag behind Hindu and Sikh organisations.

What this proves, if Bhide reproduced the newspaper faithfully: explicit Muslim press anxiety about the recruitment programme, including acknowledgement of Mahasabha activity. This is a particularly close match for the requested evidence of visible frustration.

Two necessary attribution distinctions: first, the original newspaper issue, date and author have not been recovered. The February 1943 date belongs to Bhide’s chapter, not to a verified newspaper issue. Second, the conclusion on p.28 that Jinnah’s threats had been silenced and he was reduced to asking for Pakistan is Bhide’s own commentary after the newspaper quotation ends. It must not be put in the newspaper’s mouth.

Keer calls the paper a prominent League paper; Bhide calls it a prominent Lahore Muslim newspaper. The quoted passage itself criticises Jinnah’s boasting as well as Congress and the Ahrars. Consequently, it is safest to say “Muslim newspaper opposition as reproduced by Bhide,” rather than treat every sentence as an official League resolution.

3. Toosy: preserving a military advantage as an argument for Pakistan

S3 is the primary book Pakistan and Muslim India, written under the initials M.R.T., identified by Dhulipala as Muhammad Sharif Toosy. The accessible copy has Jinnah’s foreword and the Home Study Circle’s Bombay imprint. Dhulipala dates first publication to 1942; the archive catalogues this copy as 1945. This dossier does not silently assign the accessible scan to the first edition.

On printed p.14, the book argues that the northwestern region’s inhabitants have military traditions and supply more than half the Indian army’s strength, making that region especially capable of defending independence. On p.32 it envisages a united India whose army, navy and air force would be recruited from all communities but have a preponderant Hindu element. It warns that the Muslims and Sikhs of the North-West would lose their present privileged position in the army, and that their share could fall to one-tenth or less.

Precision gained from the original: that last passage concerns the combined northwestern Muslim and Sikh contribution. Dhulipala’s p.163 paraphrase speaks more broadly of reducing Muslim military strength. Use the original’s more precise wording when citing the numerical warning.

Printed pp.212–213 supply a further direct connection to Savarkar. Toosy attacks Hindu leaders’ attempts to secure British support against Pakistan, and explicitly places the Mahasabha’s cooperation with the Viceroy and Savarkar’s appeals for Hindu militarisation in that strategy. Thus this is not merely a generic discussion of national defence.

Connection with the League: Dhulipala, pp.154–155, traces Jinnah’s commissioning and promotion of the books, publication work by the League office, Jinnah’s foreword, and their subsequent distribution and translation. That makes Toosy relevant evidence of pro-Pakistan propaganda associated with Jinnah. It does not turn every proposition in the book into a formal League resolution.

The companion Nationalism in Conflict in India (S4, pp.246–247 and surrounding discussion) argues that Britain would endanger support from Muslim recruiting regions if it alienated the League, while Congress could not match its potential military contribution. The accessible scan has discontinuities, so a long verbatim quotation should await a better copy.

Jinnah’s further use of the military argument, 1942 and 1945

Wolpert, Shameful Flight, p.47 (S15), describes Jinnah’s press conference of 13 September 1942. In the course of defending Pakistan and warning about the consequences of a confrontation with the League, Jinnah invoked an asserted Muslim share of nearly 65 per cent of the army and support from Muslim countries. Wolpert’s chapter notes point to Transfer of Power II, pp.956 and 958. This is useful evidence of military leverage in Pakistan advocacy, though the archival text still requires direct checking and the percentage must be attributed to Jinnah.

Dhulipala p.176 note 157 (S12) identifies another occasion: Jinnah’s interview to the Associated Press of America on 8 November 1945, reproduced in Jamil-ud-din Ahmad’s collection, volume II, p.432. Jinnah invoked the Punjab’s large contribution to the Indian army and the Muslim character of much of that contribution as grounds for confidence in Pakistan’s defence. This is supporting evidence of reliance on the military asset, rather than a specific objection to Savarkar. It also shows why one should distinguish opposition to militarisation from an admission that Pakistan had become hopeless.

4. Savarkar’s purpose in his own words

The 6 October 1941 statement (S5, printed pp.4–5) explicitly presents military cooperation as a way to obtain training, defend homes against external attack or internal anti-Hindu disorder, and secure a dominant Hindu position in the services where he says Muslims then predominated. This is direct evidence that altering the military balance was an intended outcome.

The Cawnpore address of December 1942 (S6, digital pp.124–126) is even closer to the Pakistan question. It first calls for mobilisation to resist attempts to impose Pakistan, then immediately proposes greatly intensified enlistment in the army, navy, air force, ammunition factories and military technical work. He explicitly wants the Muslim proportion brought down to approximately its population share. This sequence makes the connection textual, rather than something inferred solely from later biographies.

The argument does not depend on accepting the speech’s claimed 62-to-32 percentage change. Those numbers establish what Savarkar claimed and sought; they are not, by themselves, a verified statistical series.

5. Liaquat’s complaint: strong on representation, indirect on Savarkar

In S7, the entry for 8 April 1947 records that a letter from Liaquat was read at the Viceroy’s staff meeting. He complained of inadequate Muslim representation in the armed forces and wanted immediate reorganisation so they could more readily be divided between Pakistan and Hindustan. The full page also records Ismay’s objection to prejudging the political issue and Mountbatten’s resistance to splitting the army before British withdrawal.

This is concrete evidence that Muslim military representation and the future Pakistan army remained strategic concerns at the highest League level. The entry does not name Savarkar or credit his recruitment campaign with causing the situation. Keer’s narrative connects these events; the diary itself establishes only the complaint and proposed remedy.

Ayesha Jalal (S13, p.38 note 32) separately cites Liaquat’s letter of 7 April 1947 in Transfer of Power, volume X, p.152, for the assertion that Pakistan without an army would collapse like a house of cards. The archival letter should be read before treating her brief citation as its full content.

6. Contemporary Hindi evidence and the Marathi record

Rajendra Prasad: a constitutional provision to preserve communal army shares

The 1946 Hindi खण्डित भारत (S8) reproduces Sir Sikandar Hayat Khan’s scheme, introduced on printed p.289. Clause 18 on printed p.294 states that the army’s composition should remain as on 1 January 1937 and that peacetime changes in total strength should preserve each community’s proportion. It permits departures for defence emergencies or other unavoidable reasons. The page image, not just its OCR, was checked.

This is a concrete contemporaneous example of protecting existing military representation through a proposed constitutional rule. It is valuable context for the concern about dilution. However, Sikandar’s all-India federal plan and his complex relationship with the League must be distinguished from Jinnah’s sovereign-Pakistan programme. The clause is not evidence of a wartime protest against Savarkar.

Vijaykumar Pujari: December 1946 Hindi explanation of militarisation

कांग्रेस-लीग और हिन्दू महासभा (S9) is contemporary Hindi political advocacy. Its title page specifies December 1946. The chapter on militarisation, pp.199–207, especially pp.203–204, explains the call for young Hindus to enter all three services and the opportunity to acquire military training. It presents cooperation as motivated by Hindu interests and observes that Britain was likewise acting in its own interest.

This contributes a genuinely contemporary vernacular explanation of the policy. It is not an additional independent Muslim protest: the military chapter inspected does not supply one. Its partisan judgments about Gandhi, Congress and Muslims should be identified as the author’s advocacy.

Marathi collected works: authorial retrospective testimony

Printed p.471 of समग्र सावरकर वाङ्मय, खंड ८ (S10), in the Abhinav Bharat concluding-ceremony account, discusses gaining military training during the war and claims deliberate changes in the forces’ communal balance. It substantiates how Savarkar later described his policy. Because this is retrospective, it is weaker for dating a particular wartime reaction than the 1942 Jinnah collection.

Urdu: a specific publication trail, but no authenticated quotation yet

Dhulipala p.155 note 102 identifies an Urdu translation of Toosy as Pakistan aur Musalman, Delhi, 1944, translated by Muhammad Zafar Ahmad Ansari. He also documents advertising of these volumes and Urdu translations during the 1944 talks and the 1945–46 election campaign. This is a specific vernacular lead, not a vague suggestion to search Urdu someday. I did not obtain and verify the relevant Urdu pages in this pass. A similarly titled Rekhta listing by another author should not be mistaken for it.

7. Important leads that remain incomplete

  1. Ziauddin Ahmad’s Poona speech: Keer p.259 reports his alarm at increasing Hindu entry and declining Muslim percentage. The original newspaper, date and transcript remain unidentified. Report it as Keer’s account, not as an independently authenticated verbatim quotation.
  2. Eastern Times original: the strongest next step is the 1942–early 1943 run, since Bhide’s chapter is dated February 1943. A 2014 survey of historical research resources reports holdings at Punjab Public Library, Lahore; actual availability requires confirmation. Ahmad Saeed’s The Eastern Times on Quaid-i-Azam (Islamabad: NIHCR, 1983), xxviii + 316 pages, is another specific collection to consult.
  3. Lumley’s confidential assessment: a Marathi article, “British Reports on Savarkar” (The Focus India, 28 May 2022), points toward Vikram Sampath’s A Contested Legacy, p.348, and a Patel papers file titled “Congress Affairs 40–42,” National Archives of India, PP_000000005413. Its retelling incorrectly places Wavell as Viceroy during the 1942 Cripps episode. The underlying file was not authenticated here, so the colourful wording is not treated as verified British testimony.
  4. The claim of four League objections between 1941 and 1944: it appears in modern Hindi writing, including a Mahurkar/Pandit book extract. Without four separately dated primary references, it should not be presented as an established count. Liaquat’s verified 1947 complaint cannot itself verify an objection within 1941–1944.

8. A formulation the evidence can sustain

Savarkar explicitly sought to enlarge Hindu representation in the armed forces and connected military organisation with resistance to Pakistan. Jinnah recognised that purpose and publicly attacked Savarkar and Moonje’s programme in December 1941, describing Hindu military control as an obstacle to independent Muslim statehood. A contemporary Mahasabha account reproduces Muslim press alarm at Hindu and Sikh recruitment, while Jinnah-associated pro-Pakistan writing warned against dilution of existing northwestern military representation.

For the emotional claim, “visible hostility, anxiety and opposition” follows the surviving language closely. A claim that Pakistan supporters generally conceded that their hopes had become very dim goes beyond these passages. Jinnah continued to profess confidence, and Toosy used the danger of military dilution as an argument for securing Pakistan.

9. Additional primary records: Home Guards, army control and Bengal, 1942–1947

This section consolidates the five episodes previously supplied in a separate supplement into the original dossier. The two speeches on 21 September 1942 are treated as one debate; Ahsan’s covering letter and enclosures are treated as one documentary package. They add distinct evidence rather than additional publications of the Nagpur speech.

9.1 Suhrawardy and Tamizuddin Khan: Home Guard controversy, 21 September 1942

Primary source: Bengal Legislative Assembly Proceedings, volume LXIII, part 1, debate on the Chief Minister’s Statement, 21 September 1942. H. S. Suhrawardy, pp. 172–173; Tamizuddin Khan, pp. 188–189.

Suhrawardy alleged that the Home Guard circular excluded Muslim Leaguers while directing consultation with the Hindu Mahasabha:

Special directions were given not to take Muslim Leaguers but to consult the Hindu Mahasabha.

He acknowledged that the circular had apparently been changed and demanded production of both versions. He also opposed pressure on firearm licence holders to join the Home Guards or surrender their weapons.

In the same debate, Tamizuddin Khan expressed distrust of the organisation, said Muslim Leaguers were not joining, and defended the creation of the League’s National Guards. He warned that cancelling the licences of people refusing to join would be treated as a challenge to the Muslim community. He nevertheless described the idea of Home Guards as excellent if freed of the motives and arrangements he criticised.

Use: Direct, dated League criticism of a defence organisation allegedly favouring Mahasabha participation. Limit: The government was Fazlul Huq’s coalition, with Mookerjee as a minister. Its stated scheme was open to both communities. This is opposition to alleged partisan control and compulsory participation, not an explicit demand that Hindus be denied army recruitment. Suhrawardy’s allegation is evidence of what he said; the original circular has not been independently recovered here.

Suhrawardy, printed p. 173 · Tamizuddin, printed pp. 188–189

9.2 Firoz Khan Noon to Jinnah, 12 April 1947

Primary source: Z. H. Zaidi, ed., Quaid-i-Azam Mohammad Ali Jinnah Papers: Prelude to Pakistan, 20 February–2 June 1947, first series, volume I, part I (National Archives of Pakistan, 1993), document 312, p. 527. Archival reference F. 399/164–165.

Noon feared the Viceroy might prolong negotiations until British withdrawal, thereby:

handing over the army to the Hindus?

He immediately linked this danger to the need for an immediate decision on Pakistan and suggested refusing further proceedings if delay continued.

Use: An explicit private connection between Hindu control of the army and the urgency of securing Pakistan. Limit: It concerns transfer of military control, not a protest against a particular Hindu recruiting drive.

Document 312, p. 527

9.3 Raghib Ahsan’s memorandum package to Jinnah, 13 April 1947

Primary source: Jinnah Papers, I/1, document 314 and its three enclosures, pp. 528–546. Covering letter F. 204/352; enclosure 1 F. 1062/14–24; enclosure 2 F. 1062/25–29; enclosure 3 F. 1062/11–13.

The Calcutta District Muslim League figure sent Jinnah plans for military organisation, practical measures, and a letter to Suhrawardy about putting Bengal’s police on a war footing. The entire package is counted as one documentary episode.

Enclosure 1, p. 530, argues that Pakistan provinces would not be secure if Hindus controlled the wider subcontinent’s forces. It goes much further than negotiating Pakistan: it envisages an all-India struggle for military supremacy. Enclosure 2, p. 540, calls for intelligence on the Indian Army and the preparations of Hindu and Sikh states, Congress governments and organisations.

Enclosure 3, dated 13 April, pp. 544–546, treats the Hindu movement for Bengal’s partition as a potential civil-war threat. It asks for Muslim military veterans to reorganise the police and proposes:

immediate recruitment of at least 30,000 Muslim ex-servicemen

The proposal expressly responds to the perceived Gurkha danger. It includes an allocation of the proposed force among Calcutta and other industrial or strategic areas.

Use: Particularly substantial evidence of a League figure’s anxiety about Hindu/non-Muslim armed power, and a concrete plan to counter it with Muslim military manpower. Limit: These were Ahsan’s submissions. Their presence in Jinnah’s papers does not establish Jinnah’s endorsement, adoption as League policy, or implementation. No passage inspected here names Savarkar.

Covering letter and memorandum, pp. 529–530 · Practical measures, p. 540 · Letter to Suhrawardy, pp. 544–545

9.4 Mirza Ahmad Ispahani to Jinnah, 28 April 1947

Primary source: Jinnah Papers, I/1, document 356, pp. 621–622; F. 310/58–60. This is Mirza Ahmad Ispahani, not his brother M. A. H. (Hassan) Ispahani.

Ispahani alleged that the Congress–Mahasabha leadership opposed the recruitment of Punjabi policemen because it frustrated their plans. He wanted the Punjabi force doubled. He then warned that the Gurkha military police could seize Bengal’s government if the army did not intervene, and demanded:

to liquidate this force in the next 12 months.

Here the reference is to eliminating or disbanding the force as an institution; the passage should not be converted into an instruction to kill its personnel. He also expressed fears about Sikh control of transport and a possible Sikh–Nepalese combination after British withdrawal.

Use: Explicit private anxiety about non-Muslim armed predominance, including the prospect of losing political power. Limit: This concerns Bengal police composition, not an explicit criticism of Savarkar’s army enlistment campaign.

Document 356, pp. 621–622

9.5 Suhrawardy to Liaquat Ali Khan, 21 May 1947

Primary source: Jinnah Papers, I/1, enclosure 1 to document 479, pp. 867–871, particularly p. 870; F. 458/75–79. Liaquat forwarded the letters to Jinnah on 24 May, F. 335/285.

Arguing against Bengal’s partition and for an agreed united Bengal arrangement, Suhrawardy complained about the troops being sent to the province:

It is all a Hindu army.

He alleged that Muslim troops were not being sent, and feared that the army would act against them. The surrounding argument anticipates devastating violence against both West Bengal Muslims and East Bengal Hindus, and economic weakness in the resulting eastern Muslim state.

Use: Direct expression of a prominent Pakistan leader’s anxiety about Hindu military predominance in a specific deployment and its consequences for Bengal’s political future. Limit: His description is an allegation about troops deployed in Bengal, not a reliable numerical description of the entire Indian Army. It is not attributed to Savarkar’s activities.

Date and forwarding context, p. 867 · Military passage, p. 870

10. Further statements recovered: regimental composition and Pakistan’s military future

These entries extend the research beyond the five episodes in section 9. Three are checked primary documents; two are statements reported in a scholarly work with specific contemporary newspaper references. A new date means a distinct statement, not independent corroboration of every allegation made in it.

10.1 Hassan Ispahani to Jinnah, 16 March 1947: Hindu and Sikh regiments versus mixed Muslim regiments

Primary reference: S21, Jinnah Papers, I/1, document 140, pp.268–269, F.309/322–325. Written from the Indian Trade Delegation’s camp at Baghdad. The letter’s heading, date, relevant paragraph and signature were checked against the page images.

M. A. Hassan Ispahani warned Jinnah that, according to information received from an unnamed source, Baldev Singh was forming exclusively Sikh and Hindu regiments while the regiments containing Muslims remained mixed. He demanded an immediate investigation, saying:

the dangers that attend such a move are too obvious to need emphasis.

The next paragraph alleged that Congress leaders were considering a coup to take actual control of the provinces, with the central army being used after disturbances to establish their authority.

Relevance: A named senior League figure objected privately to military arrangements he believed would favour Hindu and Sikh political power. This is a particularly close addition to the question of anxiety about a changing military balance.

Limit: The letter proves Ispahani’s alarm and request for investigation. His unnamed-source information and coup allegation are not established facts merely because he communicated them to Jinnah. This is Hassan Ispahani, distinct from Mirza Ahmad Ispahani in section 9.4. The letter does not name Savarkar.

Open letter, printed pp.268–269

10.2 Firoz Khan Noon’s public demand, reported 4 April 1947

Recovered reference: S22, Hasan-Askari Rizvi, Military, State and Society in Pakistan (2000), p.52, note 53 on p.255, citing The Statesman, 4 April 1947.

Rizvi reports that Noon demanded division of the armed forces, ordnance factories and military equipment before independence, on the ground that:

whosoever gets the army will get India

Relevance: This states the military-power rationale for ensuring that Pakistan acquired its own forces. It is a different statement from Noon’s private letter of 12 April 1947, already in section 9.2.

Verification limit: Rizvi’s passage and footnote were inspected; the original newspaper issue has not been recovered here. 4 April is the cited newspaper date, not an independently established date of delivery. Do not present this as a newly authenticated verbatim newspaper transcription. Noon’s two statements are two occasions by the same speaker, not two independent witnesses.

Rizvi p.52 · Notes 52–53, p.255

10.3 Liaquat Ali Khan to Mountbatten, 13 April 1947: Hindu control could foreclose a balanced Pakistan force

Primary reference: S23, Jinnah Papers, I/1, annex V to document 420, pp.758–759. Letter No.26-P.S.F.R./47, New Delhi, 13 April 1947. Also reproduced in The Transfer of Power, volume X, document 135, pp.220–221.

Liaquat clarified that he was asking for adequate Muslim representation in every branch and reorganisation permitting later division, rather than immediate partition of the forces. His fear was that demobilisation and removal of British personnel would close the opportunity for reorganisation; Muslim personnel would remain scattered while control passed to Hindus. He warned that later hopes of creating a balanced force for Pakistan would then be futile.

The control of the forces will be in Hindu hands.

He demanded an immediate plan for military partition and asked what measures would correct inadequate Muslim representation.

Relevance: A direct connection between concern over Hindu military control and Pakistan’s future ability to obtain viable armed forces.

Limit: This is a distinct follow-up to the 7 April letter already represented in the dossier. It adds a substantive argument and new wording, but belongs to the same correspondence and must not be counted as an unrelated independent episode. The printed running heading says May 1947 because it is an annex to a May document; the letter itself is unequivocally dated 13 April. No causal attribution to Savarkar is made.

Open dated letter, pp.758–759

10.4 Complaint from Quetta forwarded to Jinnah, 19 April 1947: resisting dilution of the Baluch Regiment’s Muslim share

Primary reference: S24, Jinnah Papers, I/1, document 330 and enclosure, pp.571–576. Covering letter F.472/18; enclosure F.472/19–22. M. Ziaud Din, Barrister-at-Law, Alexandra Hotel, Quetta, 19 April 194[7].

Ziaud Din forwarded a statement by an unnamed Muslim army officer in Quetta and asked Jinnah to act on the dangerous situation it described. The memorandum contrasts all-Hindu and all-Sikh units with mixed units containing Muslims and objects to a proposed change in the Baluch Regiment from 75% Muslim to 50% Muslim through the introduction of a 25% Sikh element.

In paragraph 16, p.574, the officer describes his reaction:

it is agonising to see the ground being cut away from under our feet.

He wanted the recipient to alert senior League leaders. On p.575 he argues that the existing composition would make several battalions immediately available to act, whereas the proposed composition might leave none. He urges a vigorous public campaign over the Baluch change, including propaganda directed at Muslim officers and men.

Relevance: Exceptionally explicit evidence of frustration about reduction of a Muslim military concentration, a desire to preserve that concentration for political action, and an appeal to involve the League. The covering letter demonstrably carried the complaint to Jinnah.

Attribution and limits: The author of the military memorandum is unnamed; he cannot be promoted into a named League leader or treated as the League’s official spokesman. M. Ziaud Din is identified in the source as a barrister, not as Sir Ziauddin Ahmad of AMU. Do not identify the two without evidence. The memorandum’s statistics and accusations against Baldev Singh are its author’s claims, not independently validated findings. It does not mention Savarkar.

Covering letter, p.571 · Complaint and appeal for a campaign, pp.574–575

10.5 Nawab Muhammad Ismail Khan’s dissent on armed-forces nationalisation, reported May 1947

Recovered reference: S25, Rizvi, Military, State and Society in Pakistan, p.52, note 52 on p.255, citing The Statesman, 22 May 1947.

Rizvi records that Nawab Muhammad Ismail Khan, the Muslim member of the Armed Forces Nationalization Committee, objected to its report for completely ignoring the issue of partition. This supplies an additional named League leader resisting military planning that assumed a united India.

Relevance: The objection concerns protecting the military implications of Pakistan against a process of nationalisation that proceeded without accommodating partition.

Verification limit: This entry rests on the inspected scholarly account and its precise newspaper reference. The original dissent and newspaper issue have not been recovered. No verbatim words are attributed to Ismail Khan here, and no claim is made that his dissent specifically attacked Hindu recruitment or named Savarkar. The newspaper date is not automatically the date the dissent was written.

Rizvi’s account, p.52 · Contemporary newspaper references, p.255

How these additions should be used

The newly checked Hassan Ispahani letter and Quetta memorandum are closest to the question of opposition to a changing communal military balance. Liaquat’s follow-up explicitly connects Hindu control with the difficulty of equipping Pakistan. Noon’s public statement and Ismail Khan’s dissent widen the set of political voices, but their original newspaper texts still require recovery. None supplies a newly verified admission that Savarkar’s recruitment alone made Pakistan impossible.

Verification update, 18 October 2026: Additional Jinnah Papers correspondence was downloaded and searched; the critical passages and dates above were checked on page images. The relevant pages and footnotes in Rizvi were inspected. Searches also covered an additional contemporary Annual Register volume and English, Hindi, Marathi, Urdu and Bengali search terms. The register did not supply the missing original Noon or Ismail text in the passages searched. No new OCR engine was run.

Method and limitations

This pass used web searches in English, Hindi, Marathi and Urdu; Internet Archive metadata and title searches; downloads of book scans and their available OCR; local full-text searches; and visual inspection of the critical pages. Existing OCR was used as a finding aid, not silently treated as a faithful transcript. No new OCR engine was run. Some vernacular scans have faulty text layers; selected pages were read visually. The work is a substantial targeted survey, not an exhaustive census of every newspaper or archive.

Book copies with the same text were not counted as independent evidence. Newspaper quotations, later biographies and their underlying sources are explicitly distinguished. The main advances are the January 1942 Jinnah scan, Bhide’s fuller Eastern Times quotation, Toosy’s original warning and explicit criticism of Savarkar, the Hindi constitutional clause, and the checked Liaquat diary entry.
